# Analysis: Curator: Efficient Indexing for Multi-Tenant Vector Databases

## TECHNICAL SUMMARY

The paper proposes **Curator (arxiv:2401.07119)**, an in-memory ANN index for **multi-tenant vector databases**. The core structure is a shared **Global Clustering Tree (GCT)** plus tenant-specific **Tenant Clustering Trees (TCTs)** encoded as subtrees of the GCT (§3, Fig. 4). The GCT is a hierarchical k-means tree trained once on a representative training set; “the structure of GCT remains fixed as vectors are inserted to or deleted from the index” (§4.2). The tenant-specific tree is defined as \(TCT(t)=\{n\in N\mid t\in BF(n)\}\) in Table 1, where each node stores a **Bloom filter** \(BF(n)\) indicating which tenants have descendant shortlists, and leaf nodes may store **shortlists** \(SL(n,t)\), i.e., per-tenant lists of accessible vectors in that cluster (§3, Table 1). The search procedure is two-stage (§4.1, Algorithm 1): (1) best-first traversal over the GCT/TCT using node-centroid distances to identify candidate tenant clusters; (2) scan shortlisted vectors in candidate clusters until enough candidates are collected. Search is controlled by hyperparameters \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\) (§4.1, Algorithm 1 lines 5, 22), where traversal continues until shortlist mass reaches \(\gamma_1\gamma_2 k\), then shortlist scanning continues until \(\gamma_1k\) candidates are found.

Insertion/granting access greedily routes a vector to its nearest GCT leaf, updates or creates shortlists, and splits overfull shortlists down the tree (§4.3, Fig. 6). Deletion/revocation removes vectors from shortlists, may recompute Bloom filters upward, and may merge shortlists upward when subtree mass falls below threshold (§4.4, Fig. 7). Implementation is “approximately 1000 lines of C++ on top of the faiss library,” using OpenMP and a C++ Bloom filter library (§5).

Datasets are synthetic multi-tenant constructions over two public corpora (§6.1, Table 2): **YFCC100M**, reduced to **1M images** embedded by **CLIP** with **192-d vectors**, with **1000 tenants** defined by the top-1000 tags; and **arXiv**, reduced to **2M papers** embedded from abstracts with **all-MiniLM-L6-v2** into **384-d vectors**, with **100 tenants** defined by the 100 largest categories. Access control is generated by tag/category membership (§6.1), with average sharing degree **13.37** and **9.93** respectively (Table 2).

Metrics include **average query latency**, **P99 latency** (e.g., Figs. 8, 10, 12), **index size** (Fig. 11), **throughput** under inter-query parallelism (Fig. 9b), and recall-constrained latency / recall-vs-latency curves (Fig. 15). The main baselines are IVF-Flat and HNSW in both **metadata filtering** and **per-tenant** forms (§6.1). Configurations are selected via “grid search” to achieve “recall of at least 95%” (§6.2). Key reported results: on YFCC100M, Curator is **32.9× faster than MF-IVF** and **7.7× faster than MF-HNSW**; on arXiv, **54.6×** and **40.3×** faster respectively (§6.2). Memory is reported as **7.9–8.7× lower than PT-HNSW** and **5.9–7.6× lower than PT-IVF** while close to metadata filtering (Fig. 11, §6.2). Curator is comparable to PT-IVF in query latency but **3.3–4.7× slower than PT-HNSW** (§6.2).

## CORE CLAIM

“**Our evaluation based on the YFCC100M and arXiv datasets shows that, Curator delivers search performance on par with per-tenant indexing, while maintaining a memory footprint at the same level as metadata filtering**” (Abstract; similarly §1 and §8).

## MAIN RISKS

1. **The evaluation does not isolate whether gains come from the indexing method or from a favorable synthetic tenant-construction pipeline.**  
   - Evidence: §6.1 says tenant access metadata is *generated* from tags/categories: “we grant each tenant the access to all images associated with a specific tag” for YFCC100M, and for arXiv “we assume each tenant only access papers from a few categories.”  
   - Threat to core claim: the claimed superiority for multi-tenant retrieval may depend on tenants being semantically coherent and clusterable by tags/categories, which directly favors a tenant-specific clustering-tree representation.  
   - Decision relevance: if a practitioner’s ACLs are not aligned with semantic clusters—e.g., arbitrary business permissions, document-level shares, or overlapping ad hoc groups—the “on par with per-tenant indexing” search behavior may not hold.

2. **The paper’s “on par with per-tenant indexing” claim is not supported against the strongest per-tenant baseline reported.**  
   - Evidence: §6.2 explicitly states “Curator shows comparable performance to the PT-IVF index, but is a factor of 3.3 to 4.7 slower compared to the PT-HNSW baseline.” Yet the abstract and §1 claim performance “on par with per-tenant indexing.”  
   - Threat to core claim: the headline claim is phrased against the entire strategy class “per-tenant indexing,” but the paper’s own results show a substantial gap to one of the two per-tenant baselines.  
   - Decision relevance: practitioners choosing between deployed alternatives care about the best available per-tenant design, not a weaker subtype.

3. **Search quality reporting is under-specified and statistically weak for a systems claim based on approximate search trade-offs.**  
   - Evidence: §6.2 says configurations are chosen to maintain “a recall of at least 95% through grid search,” and Fig. 15 only shows a recall-vs-latency curve on YFCC100M. No seed variation, confidence intervals, or repeated index-training variability are reported anywhere; error bars shown in Figs. 8, 10, 12 are P99 latency, not uncertainty.  
   - Threat to core claim: without variability or multiple runs, it is unclear whether reported latencies are stable under clustering initialization, data order, or hardware noise.  
   - Decision relevance: approximate ANN systems are sensitive to training and insertion order; production adoption depends on repeatable quality/latency trade-offs, not single-run point estimates.

4. **The method assumes a fixed global tree despite acknowledging distribution drift, but provides no evaluation of drift robustness.**  
   - Evidence: §4.2 states “the structure of GCT remains fixed,” and further admits “fixing the structure of the GCT makes Curator unable to adapt to the drift in the overall distribution of vectors,” assuming either stability or periodic retraining.  
   - Threat to core claim: the claimed low-memory/high-performance trade-off may degrade over time as the fixed shared tree becomes mismatched to data.  
   - Decision relevance: real vector DBs often ingest evolving embeddings, model upgrades, and changing corpora; if retraining is required, operational cost and downtime become central.

5. **The fairness of baseline tuning is not fully established because the paper uses different implementation capabilities and parallelization regimes across methods.**  
   - Evidence: §5.2 says “HNSW baselines does not support intra-query parallelism” and Fig. 9 compares Curator/IVF with multi-threaded intra-query scans against single-thread HNSW “as a reference.” Baselines are from different libraries: faiss v1.7.4 for Curator/IVF and hnswlib v0.7.0 for HNSW (§6.1).  
   - Threat to core claim: system-level speed comparisons may reflect library/runtime differences rather than intrinsic index design.  
   - Decision relevance: adoption decisions depend on real achievable performance with available software stacks; mixing implementation constraints with method claims can mislead deployment planning.

## DOMAIN-SPECIFIC CONCERNS

1. **ACL semantics are simplified to tag/category-based group membership, which is much cleaner than real multi-tenant authorization graphs.**  
   - Evidence: YFCC100M uses “all images associated with a specific tag” as a tenant’s accessible set; arXiv assumes each tenant “only access papers from a few categories” (§6.1).  
   - Subfield concern: in vector DB deployment, ACLs are often document/user/group graphs with arbitrary overlaps, revocations, and sparse sharing, not topic clusters. Curator’s TCT idea is strongest when a tenant’s accessible vectors form coherent geometric subsets, which the chosen construction encourages.  
   - Practical implication: evaluation may overstate benefits for enterprise search or RAG systems with per-user or per-team ACLs.

2. **The shortlist design may create update hotspots under highly shared documents, but the paper does not characterize tail update cost beyond P99 latency figures.**  
   - Evidence: insertion “grant[s] access permissions to every tenant in its access list” (§6.2), and shortlist split/merge cascades are described in §4.3–§4.4; §6.2 notes “P99 latency for insertion and deletion is significantly higher… because some updates requires significant modifications… (e.g., merging and spliting of shortlists in Curator).”  
   - Subfield concern: ACL-heavy vector stores frequently face bursty sharing/revocation events; tree-local split/merge behavior can amplify write tails.  
   - Practical implication: operational SLOs can be dominated by tail writes, especially for collaborative sharing workloads.

3. **False positives from Bloom filters are claimed to preserve correctness, but the paper does not quantify the induced traversal overhead as tenant count or sharing degree grows.**  
   - Evidence: §4.1 says Bloom false positives “does not compromise the correctness… only has a minor effect on search efficiency,” and §4.4 similarly says batched stale Bloom filters “only slightly affects performance.” Fig. 13b studies memory scaling with tenant count but not search degradation from Bloom filter saturation.  
   - Subfield concern: in multi-tenant indexes, filter occupancy and false-positive rate can materially affect traversal cost.  
   - Practical implication: if tenant counts or sharing patterns exceed the tested regime, the search-performance claim may erode.

4. **The paper evaluates only in-memory operation, yet compares itself to practical vector DB multi-tenancy strategies often deployed with disk, compression, or hybrid serving.**  
   - Evidence: Abstract and §1 call Curator “an in-memory vector index”; §6.1 says “For all indexes, we store vector in their original representation without scalar or product quantization.”  
   - Subfield concern: production vector DBs frequently use PQ/SQ, disk-backed indexes, and memory-tiering. The claimed memory-performance frontier may shift materially under compressed or out-of-core settings.  
   - Practical implication: practitioners cannot infer whether the reported advantages survive realistic storage constraints.

5. **Deletion fairness is partially sidestepped by changing the compared operation for HNSW.**  
   - Evidence: §6.2 states “To ensure fairness, we compare the performance of vector deletion with IVF baselines, and the performance of vector update with HNSW baselines.”  
   - Subfield concern: systems users still need deletion semantics across all candidate indexes; replacing deletion with update for one family changes the question.  
   - Practical implication: if a deployment requires true delete-heavy ACL revocation, the comparative story remains incomplete.

## STRENGTHS

- **The paper presents a concrete data structure and algorithmic decomposition rather than only a systems heuristic.** The GCT/TCT/shortlist/Bloom filter formulation is explicit in §3, Table 1, and Algorithm 1.
- **The design addresses both query-time filtering cost and per-tenant memory overhead in one mechanism.** §3 explicitly reorganizes access lists into shortlists and encodes tenant trees via Bloom filters.
- **The implementation details and API surface are reasonably concrete.** §5 states the code base is “approximately 1000 lines of C++ on top of the faiss library,” lists APIs, and names external dependencies.
- **The evaluation includes multiple operation types, not only search.** §6.2 reports query, insertion, deletion, update, memory, and multi-threaded throughput results (Figs. 8–12).
- **The paper includes an ablation that decomposes gains from Bloom filters, shortlists, and best-first search.** §6.4 and Fig. 14 isolate +BF, +SL, and +BFS.
- **The authors explicitly acknowledge a key limitation of the fixed GCT under distribution drift.** §4.2 states this design “makes Curator unable to adapt to the drift in the overall distribution of vectors.”

## WEAKNESSES

- **The main “on par with per-tenant indexing” claim overreaches the reported evidence.** §6.2 says Curator is “3.3 to 4.7 slower compared to the PT-HNSW baseline,” contradicting the broad wording in the abstract and §1.
- **Tenant access patterns are synthetically derived from semantic tags/categories rather than real ACL logs.** §6.1 defines tenants by YFCC tags and arXiv categories, which may structurally favor tenant-specific clustering.
- **No uncertainty estimates or repeated-run robustness are provided for core claims.** §6 reports point estimates and P99 latency bars, but no seed variance, confidence intervals, or repeated training runs.
- **Baseline fairness is blurred by library and feature differences.** §6.1 uses faiss for IVF/Curator and hnswlib for HNSW; §5.2 notes HNSW lacks intra-query parallelism support in the tested implementation.
- **The fixed-GCT limitation is acknowledged but not experimentally probed.** §4.2 admits inability to adapt to drift, yet no drift experiment appears in §6.
- **Recall-quality reporting is incomplete across datasets and baselines.** Fig. 15 gives recall-vs-latency only on YFCC100M; §6.2 only states tuning to “at least 95%” recall without per-method achieved recalls.
- **The paper claims shortlists add “no additional memory overhead,” but does not provide a formal accounting or breakdown table.** §3 states this directly, while Fig. 11 only shows aggregate index sizes.
- **The deletion comparison avoids direct HNSW deletion comparison by substituting update.** §6.2 changes the measured operation for HNSW because of deferred deletion semantics.
- **The dataset subsampling and tenant truncation are constrained by host memory, limiting scale claims.** §6.1 uses 1M YFCC images, 2M arXiv vectors, and only the top-1000 / top-100 categories “so that… indexes can fit into the memory of our server.”
- **The paper does not report the actual Bloom filter configuration or false-positive rates despite relying on them for both correctness arguments and memory efficiency.** Bloom filters are central in §3–§4, but parameterization is omitted from implementation/evaluation sections.

## FORENSIC DEEP-DIVE

### Eval Gaps

#### 1. The central claim is broader than the strongest evidence supports.
- **Paper evidence:** Abstract: Curator “delivers search performance on par with per-tenant indexing.” §6.2: “Curator shows comparable performance to the PT-IVF index, but is a factor of 3.3 to 4.7 slower compared to the PT-HNSW baseline.”
- **What it breaks:** The paper’s core claim is stated against a strategy class, but the results are only “on par” with one per-tenant baseline (PT-IVF), not the stronger PT-HNSW baseline that is also part of that class.
- **Why it matters:** For a practitioner, “per-tenant indexing” includes HNSW if they can afford memory. The paper’s own evidence says Curator is materially slower there, so the headline should be narrowed to “comparable to PT-IVF while using much less memory,” not the broader claim.

#### 2. The access-control workload is synthetic in a way that may structurally favor the method.
- **Paper evidence:** §6.1: YFCC tenants get “all images associated with a specific tag.” arXiv tenants are created assuming “each tenant only access papers from a few categories.”
- **What it breaks:** Curator’s TCT depends on each tenant having a meaningful tenant-specific vector distribution (§3, §4). The chosen construction uses semantic labels/categories to define access sets, which makes such distributions more likely than arbitrary ACLs.
- **Why it matters:** In real enterprise vector DBs, access rights are often induced by org charts, project membership, or explicit sharing, not by topic tags. If ACLs are semantically heterogeneous, the tree may not adapt as cleanly, and shortlist localization could weaken.

#### 3. Search-quality and tuning methodology are insufficiently transparent.
- **Paper evidence:** §6.2: “we select index configurations that achieve the best search performance while maintaining a recall of at least 95% through grid search.” Fig. 15 provides recall-vs-latency only on YFCC100M.
- **What it breaks:** The paper never reports the search-parameter search space, achieved recall for each method in the main latency tables, or whether the same tuning budget was applied uniformly across baselines.
- **Why it matters:** ANN performance is highly sensitive to hyperparameters. Without matched tuning disclosure, latency advantages may reflect more aggressive recall sacrifice or unequal search over baseline configurations.

### Confounds

#### 4. The comparison mixes method differences with implementation constraints.
- **Paper evidence:** §6.1 uses faiss for Curator and IVF, hnswlib for HNSW. §5.2 states HNSW “does not support intra-query parallelism” in the open-source implementation used, and Fig. 9 compares parallel Curator/IVF against single-thread HNSW “as a reference.”
- **What it breaks:** Performance claims in Fig. 9 partly measure software ecosystem capability rather than purely index-design properties.
- **Why it matters:** A systems paper should separate algorithmic claims from implementation limitations. Otherwise, a practitioner may infer that Curator intrinsically scales better than HNSW, when the reported gap is mediated by chosen libraries and unavailable features.

#### 5. Memory claims are aggregate and do not validate the “no additional memory overhead” assertion for shortlists.
- **Paper evidence:** §3 says “storing access information in shortlists does not increase memory usage, as it essentially represents the same access matrix in a different layout.” Fig. 11 only reports total index size across methods.
- **What it breaks:** This is a strong structural claim, but the paper gives no memory breakdown for vectors, centroids, Bloom filters, shortlists, labels, or per-tenant bookkeeping.
- **Why it matters:** In practice, representation changes can alter allocator overhead, pointer overhead, cache locality, and duplication. Aggregate totals are not enough to verify the mechanistic claim.

### Scope

#### 6. The method admits a non-trivial drift limitation without corresponding evaluation.
- **Paper evidence:** §4.2: “fixing the structure of the GCT makes Curator unable to adapt to the drift in the overall distribution of vectors,” relying on assumed stability or periodic retraining.
- **What it breaks:** Curator’s benefits rely on a globally trained clustering scaffold. If embeddings drift due to model updates or domain shift, both tenant subtree structure and search efficiency can degrade.
- **Why it matters:** This is not a corner case: production embedding systems routinely rotate models and ingest new domains. The omission leaves operational viability unresolved.

#### 7. Update tail behavior is acknowledged but not characterized in enough depth.
- **Paper evidence:** §6.2 says “P99 latency for insertion and deletion is significantly higher… because some updates requires significant modifications… (e.g., merging and spliting of shortlists in Curator).”
- **What it breaks:** The paper claims high insertion/deletion efficiency in the abstract, but tail-heavy update costs may violate service-level objectives even if averages look good.
- **Why it matters:** Multi-tenant systems often operate under strict p99 write or permission-update SLOs; averages are not the decisive metric.

### Math & Logic Errors

#### 8. There is an apparent inconsistency in Algorithm 1 variable naming/termination logic.
- **Paper evidence:** Algorithm 1 line 5 uses “while frontier ≠ ∅ and **nCand** < \(\gamma_1\gamma_2k\)” but line 4 defines **nVecs ← 0** as “Total size of shortlists.” The update in line 11 increments nVecs, not nCand.
- **What it breaks:** The pseudocode, as written, has a termination variable mismatch. Presumably the loop should compare nVecs, not nCand.
- **Why it matters:** This is small but relevant for reproducibility; termination criteria directly affect search quality/latency trade-offs.

## MISSING EVALUATIONS

1. **Real ACL distribution evaluation.**  
   - Missing experiment: Evaluate on a dataset with actual user/group/document sharing logs rather than synthetic tag/category-derived access sets.  
   - Claim tested: the abstract claim that Curator supports multi-tenancy efficiently in realistic settings.  
   - Why decision-relevant: practitioners need evidence beyond semantically aligned synthetic ACLs.

2. **Distribution-drift / embedding-version-shift evaluation.**  
   - Missing experiment: Train GCT on one distribution, then ingest shifted data or embeddings from an updated encoder; measure latency/recall degradation and retraining cost.  
   - Claim tested: §4.2’s operational assumption that fixed GCT remains adequate with periodic retraining.  
   - Why decision-relevant: production vector DBs regularly face corpus and model drift.

3. **Per-method achieved recall and matched-tuning disclosure for all main results.**  
   - Missing experiment/reporting: For Figs. 8–12, report achieved recall per method/configuration and the tuning budget/search space.  
   - Claim tested: whether latency comparisons are quality-matched.  
   - Why decision-relevant: ANN trade-offs are only meaningful at matched recall.

4. **Sensitivity to Bloom filter false-positive rate and tenant count.**  
   - Missing experiment: vary Bloom filter size/hash count and tenant counts; report false-positive rates and resulting query overhead.  
   - Claim tested: §4.1 and §4.4 assertions that false positives have only “minor” or “slight” performance effect.  
   - Why decision-relevant: production tenant counts can exceed tested settings.

5. **Memory breakdown by component.**  
   - Missing experiment/reporting: decompose total memory into vector storage, tree nodes, centroids, Bloom filters, shortlists, labels, and metadata.  
   - Claim tested: §3’s statement that shortlists add “no additional memory overhead” and the broader low-memory claim.  
   - Why decision-relevant: operators need to understand where memory goes to plan capacity.

6. **Delete/revoke-heavy workload with tail distributions.**  
   - Missing experiment: bursty access grants/revocations and high-sharing vectors; report p95/p99/p999 update latencies.  
   - Claim tested: abstract claim of high insertion and deletion efficiency.  
   - Why decision-relevant: collaborative multi-tenant systems often have spiky write/update patterns.

7. **Compressed / disk-backed baseline comparison.**  
   - Missing experiment: compare against PQ/SQ or disk-backed indexes under similar memory budgets.  
   - Claim tested: memory-performance trade-off relevance for practical vector databases.  
   - Why decision-relevant: in-memory raw-vector-only comparisons may not reflect deployment choices.

## SHARPEST FLAW

The sharpest flaw is that the paper’s headline claim overstates what its own experiments show: the abstract and §1 repeatedly claim Curator achieves search performance “on par with per-tenant indexing,” but §6.2 states that Curator is only “comparable” to **PT-IVF** and is still “a factor of **3.3 to 4.7 slower** compared to the **PT-HNSW** baseline.” Since PT-HNSW is itself a per-tenant indexing baseline included by the authors, the broad claim is not supported as written. This matters because practitioners choosing a multi-tenant design are comparing against the strongest viable per-tenant alternative, not only PT-IVF. The paper has a plausible memory/performance trade-off story, but the core claim should be narrowed substantially.

## ACCEPTANCE RECOMMENDATION

**Weak Reject**

**Reasoning:** The paper has a concrete design and strong systems intuition, but the central claim (“on par with per-tenant indexing”) is contradicted by §6.2’s reported 3.3–4.7× gap to PT-HNSW and the evaluation relies on synthetic ACLs constructed from semantic tags/categories (§6.1).

## DATASET & DEPLOYMENT AUDIT

### DATASETS

- **Construction bias from synthetic ACL generation.**  
  - Evidence: §6.1 says for YFCC100M “we grant each tenant the access to all images associated with a specific tag,” and for arXiv “we assume each tenant only access papers from a few categories.”  
  - Concern: these access sets are not observed tenant permissions but constructed from semantic metadata. This can induce selection artifacts where each tenant’s accessible vectors are semantically coherent and therefore easier for tenant-specific clustering trees to model.

- **Scale/distribution mismatch versus real deployment ACLs.**  
  - Evidence: §6.1 uses only “the 1000 tags that appear most frequently” for YFCC100M and “the 100 largest categories” for arXiv.  
  - Concern: restricting to most frequent tags/categories biases evaluation toward large, well-populated tenants and omits the long tail of sparse tenants that are common in real SaaS/enterprise systems.

- **Synthetic vs. real access metadata.**  
  - Evidence: §6.1 explicitly says “we generate vectors and access metadata from some well-known datasets” because “existing vector search benchmarks do not have information about tenant-specific access permissions.”  
  - Concern: the tenant labels/access control structure are synthetic, not real-world annotations. This raises circularity risk because the access sets are derived from the same semantic descriptors that shape the vector geometry.

- **Potential label-quality simplification in access control.**  
  - Evidence: YFCC access is derived from tags; arXiv access from categories (§6.1).  
  - Concern: no noise model is given for those tags/categories as a proxy for who should have access. The evaluation assumes metadata categories correspond cleanly to tenant permissions, which is a strong simplification.

- **Subsampling and truncation to fit one server.**  
  - Evidence: §6.1 uses “1M images randomly chosen from the original YFCC100M dataset” and only the top-1000 tags “so that both Curator and the baseline systems’ indexes can fit into the memory of our server.”  
  - Concern: results are constrained by a single-node memory budget, so dataset scale may not reflect larger deployments the paper motivates.

### DEPLOYMENT / PRODUCTIONIZATION

- **Inference-time dependency on tenant identity and maintained ACL structures.**  
  - Evidence: the search API is `knn_search(vector, k, tenant, params)` (§5.1), and search logic relies on `BF(n)` and `SL(n,t)` (§4.1, Algorithm 1).  
  - Concern: deployment requires every query to carry an exact tenant identifier and depends on continuously maintained tenant-specific shortlists/Bloom filters. This adds serving-path coupling to ACL state.

- **Infrastructure assumption: in-memory storage on a large-memory server.**  
  - Evidence: abstract calls Curator “an in-memory vector index”; experiments use a server with “256GB memory” (§6.1).  
  - Concern: the method is evaluated only in-memory and without compression (§6.1: “we store vector in their original representation without scalar or product quantization”), which may limit applicability for memory-constrained deployments.

- **Versioning / drift sensitivity due to fixed global tree.**  
  - Evidence: §4.2 says GCT remains fixed and admits this “makes Curator unable to adapt to the drift in the overall distribution of vectors,” assuming periodic retraining.  
  - Concern: production systems with evolving corpora or embedding-model upgrades may require re-training/rebuilding workflows that the paper does not quantify.

- **Integration complexity from multiple coupled components.**  
  - Evidence: implementation depends on faiss, OpenMP, and a separate Bloom filter library (§5); correctness/performance rely on GCT, per-tenant shortlists, Bloom filters, and access-list bookkeeping (§3–§5).  
  - Concern: production integration is more complex than dropping in a standard index because ACL updates, shortlist splits/merges, and Bloom filter recomputation must remain consistent.

- **Latency concerns for write-heavy workloads due to structural updates.**  
  - Evidence: §4.3–§4.4 describe shortlist splitting/merging and recursive Bloom filter updates; §6.2 notes significantly higher P99 due to “significant modifications to the index at runtime.”  
  - Concern: under bursty sharing/revocation, tail latency may be problematic even if average latency is acceptable.

## POINTERS

- The abstract claim that Curator is “on par with per-tenant indexing” is not supported against PT-HNSW, which §6.2 reports is still 3.3–4.7× faster.
- The tenant-access workload is synthetic: §6.1 creates YFCC tenants by granting access to “all images associated with a specific tag,” which may favor semantically coherent tenant partitions.
- The arXiv workload likewise assumes each tenant accesses “papers from a few categories” (§6.1), which is not evidence for arbitrary real ACLs.
- The evaluation only uses the top-1000 tags and top-100 categories so indexes fit “into the memory of our server” (§6.1), limiting representativeness of long-tail tenants.
- Algorithm 1 has a termination-variable inconsistency: line 5 checks `nCand < γ1·γ2·k` but line 4 defines and updates `nVecs`, not `nCand`.
- The paper asserts in §3 that shortlists incur “no additional memory overhead,” but provides no component-wise memory accounting beyond aggregate Fig. 11 totals.
- The fixed-GCT design is acknowledged to be unable “to adapt to the drift in the overall distribution of vectors” (§4.2), but no drift experiment is reported in §6.
- Search hyperparameters \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\) are tuned by grid search (§4.1, §6.2), but the paper does not disclose the search space or tuning budget for fairness.
- Fig. 15 shows recall-vs-latency only on YFCC100M, leaving the second dataset without a full quality/latency trade-off evaluation.
- The paper reports P99 latency bars in Figs. 8, 10, and 12, but provides no multi-run uncertainty estimates or seed variability for index training.
- The intra-query parallelism comparison in Fig. 9 is confounded because §5.2 states HNSW lacks such support in the tested open-source implementation.
- Baseline implementations come from different libraries—faiss for Curator/IVF and hnswlib for HNSW (§6.1)—which complicates attributing differences solely to index design.
- Deletion fairness is only partially addressed because §6.2 replaces HNSW deletion comparison with “vector update” instead of directly comparing deletion semantics.
- The paper claims Bloom filter false positives have only a “minor effect on search efficiency” (§4.1), but never reports actual false-positive rates or sensitivity to tenant count.
- Update tail costs are explicitly acknowledged in §6.2 (“P99 latency… significantly higher” due to shortlist split/merge), but no p999 or bursty-update analysis is provided.
- All experiments store vectors “in their original representation without scalar or product quantization” (§6.1), so the memory-performance results may not transfer to realistic compressed vector DB deployments.
- The benchmark inserts, searches, and deletes “sequentially without batching” (§6.2), which does not cover concurrent mixed-workload behavior typical in production systems.
- The memory-scaling study in Fig. 13b varies tenant count synthetically but does not evaluate whether Bloom-filter-driven search cost degrades at the same time.
